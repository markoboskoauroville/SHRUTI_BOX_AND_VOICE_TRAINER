How it works

The box

Nothing is recorded: every reed is synthesised on the phone. One cycle of a free-reed tone, 28 harmonics with the odd ones stronger, played as two voices a cent and a half apart for the beating of a real reed. Each valve swells in and fades out so a tap never clicks, and the bellows breathe the whole box slowly. Open more valves and the level follows, so the box never shouts.

  • Tap a valve to open it, tap again to close. RESET closes them all.
  • Each sounding note becomes a button: a tap steps it full → half → a line (0, −12, −24 dB).
  • ARP plays any chord as an arpeggio; a raga card's ▶ SCALE plays its aroha and avaroha.

The drones keep the tanpura's own logic: Sa, Sa' and one tuning note, Pa as a rule, ma, Ni or Dha when the raga asks for it. The ragas, from Yaman and Bhairav to Malkauns and Charukeshi, carry their thaat, time, mood, aroha and avaroha, and their notes are marked on the valves. The chords are Western chords played as drones.

CUSTOM is yours: whatever sounds, the save icon keeps; ✎ names it.

The sequencer

In CUSTOM, switch on SEQ and tap your cards in the order you want: each gets a number. ▶ SEQUENCE plays them round and round, one beat each, at the BPM you set right there. XFADE lets one chord melt into the next over the time you choose, so the box changes like breath, never like a switch.

The voice trainer

Play a note, close the box, and sing it back. The microphone opens only when every reed is silent, so it hears you and never the shruti. A row of oval lights is the tuner: the middle one is the note, flat to the left, sharp to the right, each light a tenth of a semitone.

  • AUTO starts at once on Sa: the box plays each note of the scale for three seconds, then you sing it; hold it close for two seconds and the next note comes.
  • Difficulty: ±20 % (hardest), ±40 % or ±60 % of a semitone. A long press on AUTO changes it.
  • Range: any stretch of the 88 piano keys, A0 to C8. The box follows each note into its octave.
  • The voice is heard frame by frame and never kept.

Play it with a MIDI keyboard

Any class-compliant USB MIDI keyboard works. The phone gives it power through the cable.

  1. Connect the keyboard to the phone with a USB-C to USB-C cable (or the keyboard's own cable and a USB OTG adapter).
  2. Open the app. Settings → MIDI shows the keyboard's name.
  3. Play. A key held opens its valve, letting go closes it; strike harder and it sounds louder. Every key sounds at its own pitch across the 88 piano keys, A0 to C8, and the box follows the octave you play.

Made for the Novation Launchkey Mini MK4

With a Launchkey connected, the app takes it over the way Ableton Live does, switching it into its DAW mode by itself. Nothing to set up on the keyboard.

  • Pads are the sequencer's steps: a pad lights when you give a preset its number, glows white while its step sounds, and a press plays that step.
  • Eight knobs set the loudness of Sa to Sa'; with Shift, the five upper valves. The display shows the note and its level.
  • Pitch strip bends the sound, modulation strip adds vibrato, both shown on the display.
  • Octave − / + on the keyboard: the box follows on the first key you play. The app's ◀ ▶ shift the keys too and show the octave on the Launchkey's display.
  • Velocity curve (soft, normal, hard, fixed) is set from the app's settings and kept by the keyboard.
  • Leave the app and it keeps playing in the background; ✕ releases everything and hands the keyboard back.

The keyboard's own Octave buttons are not reported to any app (not to Ableton either), so the app learns the octave from the next key.

Play for hours: charge while you play

The phone has one USB-C port, and the keyboard takes it. To keep the phone charging through a long kirtan or a morning practice, put a small USB-C hub with Power Delivery pass-through between them.

The story

From France to India: how a drone found its box

The free reed, a thin tongue of metal that sings when air passes it, is far older in Asia than in Europe: the Chinese sheng is named in the Shi Jing, three thousand years ago. Europe met it in the late 18th century, and by the 1840s it had become an instrument of its own.

1840 · FRANCE

Alexandre Débain receives a patent for the instrument and for its name, harmonium: free reeds, blown by foot treadles.

MID TO LATE 1800s · BENGAL

The harmonium reaches India through traders and missionaries, first in West Bengal. Indian musicians sit on the floor, so the foot pedals give way to bellows pumped by hand.

1875 · CALCUTTA

Dwarkanath Ghose founds D. Ghosh and Sons (later Dwarkin) on Lower Chitpur Road and is credited with adapting the imported instrument into the hand-pumped Indian harmonium.

THE DRONE GETS ITS OWN BOX

Before, the drone came from the tanpura. Musicians began holding harmonium keys down for a steady Sa and Pa; then a box without keys was built for the drone alone: the shruti box, also called sur peti. A keyless harmonium, only for the drone.

1971 · BANGALORE

G. Raj Narayan, an engineer and flautist, builds an electronic shruti box, and his electronic tanpura follows in 1979. A drone that never needs pumping.

TODAY · EVERYWHERE

From Indian classical practice to Irish song and the kirtan circles of the world, the shruti box holds the ground: Sa, Pa, Sa', and the voice rising over it.

Why mantras love a drone

A mantra is sound before it is meaning. A steady drone gives the voice a home to return to: you hear at once when you drift from Sa, and every syllable settles on the same ground. That is why the shruti box became the companion of mantra singing, kirtan and bhajan, and of the plain Sa–Pa–Sa practice that every Indian singer starts with. Its player can pump with one hand and sing at the same time; this app needs no hands at all.
